# India's Regulatory and Legal Framework on Tobacco/Alcohol Advertising and Surrogate Advertising

## What does COTPA 2003 and its rules require/prohibit, and what are the specific film/TV depiction rules (health warnings, disclaimers)?

### Takeaway
COTPA (2003) imposes a near-total ban on direct and indirect tobacco advertising and sponsorship (Section 5), mandates large pictorial health warnings on packaging (Section 7, 40% of principal display area), and since 2012 ("Tobacco-Free Film and TV Rules") has required films/TV depicting tobacco use to carry specific health spots, scrolling/static warnings, audiovisual disclaimers, and editorial justification; these rules were extended to OTT/streaming platforms via 2023–2024 amendments, but academic audits find compliance is poor.

### Cited Findings
- Section 5 of COTPA 2003 prohibits "direct and indirect advertisement" of tobacco products in all audio, visual and print media; bans sponsorship of sports/cultural events by tobacco companies; and prohibits any person from taking part in advertising that directly or indirectly suggests/promotes use of cigarettes or tobacco products — [Section 5, COTPA 2003 (Indian Kanoon)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/48542/)
- Penalty for a first-time violation of Section 5 is imprisonment up to 2 years and/or a fine up to Rs. 1,000; for repeat/habitual offenders, imprisonment up to 5 years and fine up to Rs. 5,000 — [Section 5, COTPA 2003 (Indian Kanoon)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/48542/)
- Section 7 of COTPA requires every tobacco product package to carry a prominent, legible, pictorial health warning in the same language as other pack information; the specified warning must occupy 40% of the principal display area of the front panel, positioned parallel to the top edge, and must not be obscured when the pack is sealed/opened — [General Guidelines for implementation of Section 7 of COTPA (Kerala VHS)](https://www.keralavhs.org/assets/pdf/SHW%20enforcement%20guide_revised%20guidelines%20for%20Section%207.pdf)
- A display board stating "sale of tobacco products to minors is prohibited" is mandated at points of sale under COTPA — [General Guidelines for implementation of Section 7 of COTPA (Kerala VHS)](https://www.keralavhs.org/assets/pdf/SHW%20enforcement%20guide_revised%20guidelines%20for%20Section%207.pdf)
- The "Tobacco-Free Film and TV Rules," notified under COTPA, came into effect October 2/November 14, 2011–2012, and require: (1) anti-tobacco health spots of minimum 30 seconds each at the beginning and middle of the film/TV programme; (2) a prominent static health-warning message (older versions used a scrolling warning) at the bottom of the screen during display of tobacco products/use; (3) an audio-visual disclaimer on the ill-effects of tobacco, minimum 20 seconds each, at the beginning and middle of the programme; and (4) a "strong editorial justification" submitted to the Central Board of Film Certification (CBFC) explaining why tobacco depiction was necessary — [PIB notification, Nov 2011](https://www.pib.gov.in/newsite/printrelease.aspx?relid=77120&reg=48&lang=2); [Mondaq: "Smoking Is Injurious To OTT Platforms"](https://www.mondaq.com/india/broadcasting-film-tv-radio/1407864/smoking-is-injurious-to-ott-platforms)
- A 2023/2024 amendment extends these same requirements (30-second non-skippable health spots, 20-second audiovisual disclaimer on opening the platform, static on-screen warnings during tobacco depiction) to OTT/streaming platforms; the draft is formally "The Cigarette and other Tobacco Products (Prohibition of Advertisement and Regulation of Trade and Commerce, Production, Supply and Distribution) Amendment Rules, 2024," building on a May 2022 Health Ministry draft, with platforms given a six-month compliance window — [Medianama coverage of final MoHFW consultation](https://www.medianama.com/2025/06/223-ott-tobacco-depiction-rules-final-consultation-mohfw/); [Internet Freedom Foundation submission on the 2024 Amendment Rules](https://internetfreedom.in/iffs-submissions-on-the-cigarettes-other-tobacco-products-amendment-rules-2024/)
- A peer-reviewed content analysis of 47 popular Indian films (English, Hindi, Malayalam, Tamil, Telugu, Tulu) found tobacco imagery in 72% of films and actual tobacco use depicted in 50%; tobacco imagery was equally common in U-rated and U/A-rated films and significantly more common in regional-language films than in Hindi; **none of the films studied were fully compliant** with the legal requirements on health spots, audiovisual disclaimers, and health warnings — [Tobacco Control journal study, PMC6952842](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6952842/)
- A trend analysis of top-grossing Bollywood films (2006–2017) found that the 2012 Tobacco-Free Film and TV Rules were followed by a measurable reduction in on-screen tobacco depiction incidents, though implementation/enforcement issues persisted — [Changes in tobacco depictions after implementation of tobacco-free film and TV rules in Bollywood films, Tobacco Control / WHO SEARO reprint](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/searo/india/health-topic-pdf/tobacco/changes-in-tobacco-depictions-after-implementation-of-tobacco-free-film-and-tv-rules-_tobacco-control.pdf)

### Inferences
- The gap between formal compliance requirements (health spots, disclaimers, static warnings, editorial justification filed with CBFC) and observed on-screen practice (per the 72%-imagery, 0%-fully-compliant finding) suggests that automated detection work should not assume health-warning overlays reliably co-occur with tobacco depiction in Indian film/TV content — many scenes depicting tobacco may lack the mandated warning entirely.
- The extension of these rules to OTT in 2023–2024 (still being finalized/pushed back against by industry per IFF and Medianama) means dataset collection timeframes matter: pre-2023 OTT content is unlikely to carry any compliance markers at all, while post-rule content may show inconsistent implementation.

### Gaps
- Exact current text/status of the finalized 2024 OTT Amendment Rules (whether notified in final form, and effective date) was not confirmed — Medianama (2025) describes continued pushback/consultation, suggesting the rule may still not be fully in force as of research date; the report-writer should flag this as an evolving regulation.
- Did not retrieve the full text of COTPA Sections 4 (prohibition of smoking in public places) or 6 (prohibition of sale to minors/near educational institutions) in detail, though these are part of the broader Act and may be relevant background for a complete picture.

## What are the state-level and national rules on alcohol advertising in India?

### Takeaway
There is no single central "alcohol advertising act"; alcohol advertising is restricted nationally on cable/TV under the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 and its 1994 Rules, and separately through state excise acts (most states ban alcohol ad promotion/sponsorship in their excise laws), creating a patchwork that is widely credited as the direct cause of surrogate advertising's rise.

### Cited Findings
- Rule 7(2)(viii) of the Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994 explicitly prohibits advertisements that directly or indirectly promote the production, sale, or consumption of wine and other alcoholic beverages on cable networks — [Lexology: "Prohibited and controlled advertising in India"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=13499538-2b53-4377-aafa-1f935f7d40b3)
- A 2009 amendment to the Cable Television Network Rules specifies that cable advertisements cannot refer to alcohol products directly, but brand names themselves can still be advertised — a distinction that directly enabled surrogate/brand-extension advertising — [WebSearch synthesis citing Mondaq "Regulating Surrogate Advertising In India"](https://www.mondaq.com/india/advertising-marketing--branding/1231928/regulating-surrogate-advertising-in-india)
- Potable alcohol regulation is primarily a state subject in India; five states/UTs (Bihar, Gujarat, Mizoram, parts of Manipur, and Nagaland) prohibit sale and consumption of alcohol entirely, and a ban on advertisement, promotion, or sponsorship of alcohol products exists in the excise acts of "all but 10" Indian states — [PLOS ONE: "Alcohol policies in India: A scoping review"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0294392)
- India's surrogate advertising phenomenon is historically traced to the mid-1990s, specifically after the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 (read with the 1994 Cable TV Rules) banned direct liquor and tobacco advertising on cable — [Mondaq: "Surrogate Advertisements In India"](https://www.mondaq.com/india/advertising-marketing-branding/606974/surrogate-advertisements-in-india)
- The Wikipedia entry on surrogate advertising (secondary/tertiary source, cross-check against primary sources) states India's advertising ban on tobacco and liquor dates to 1995, following government health research, and traces the regulatory lineage back to the 1975 Cigarette Advertising Act requirement for packaging health warnings — [Wikipedia: Surrogate advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogate_advertising)

### Inferences
- Because alcohol advertising law in India is fragmented across central cable-TV rules and inconsistent state excise acts (with roughly 10 states having no explicit ad ban in their excise acts per the PLOS scoping review), national broadcast/cable/print media still largely self-regulate alcohol advertising via the surrogate/brand-extension route governed by ASCI rather than via a uniform statute — meaning legal risk and enforcement vary by state and medium, which likely contributes to inconsistency in how alcohol-linked surrogate ads look/behave across regions.

### Gaps
- Did not find a single authoritative consolidated list of which specific states ban alcohol advertising in their excise acts versus which ~10 do not; the PLOS scoping review is the best source found but a state-by-state table was not extracted in full.
- Did not find detail on Press Council of India norms specific to print alcohol advertising, nor on radio-specific rules (All India Radio/private FM) beyond the FM surrogate-ad study noted under surrogate advertising below.

## What is "surrogate advertising" precisely, and what are documented real examples in India?

### Takeaway
Surrogate advertising is the practice of advertising a legally permitted product (soda, mineral water, cardamom/elaichi, music CDs, playing cards) using the same brand name, logo, colors, jingle, or celebrity endorsers as a legally restricted product (liquor, pan masala/gutka, cigarettes) specifically to maintain the restricted brand's visibility and recall; it is well-documented in India across liquor (Bagpiper, Officer's Choice, Imperial Blue, Bacardi, McDowell's/Kingfisher) and tobacco/pan masala (Vimal, Rajnigandha, Kamla Pasand) brands, and has recently escalated to celebrity-endorsement controversies (e.g., the 2026 Vimal Elaichi case).

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "No surrogate advertisement or indirect advertisement shall be made for goods or services whose advertising is otherwise prohibited or restricted by law, by circumventing such prohibition or restriction and portraying it to be an advertisement for other goods or services, the advertising of which is not prohibited or restricted by law" — [ASCI Code, cited via Lexology "Legal analysis of tobacco and alcohol advertising laws in India"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=c3625316-71db-492b-b896-a5bd471614ed)
- A surrogate advertisement "duplicates the brand image of one product to promote another product of the same brand" — its core function is brand recall of the restricted product via a legal proxy product, not sale of the proxy product itself — [Mondaq: "Surrogate Advertisements In India"](https://www.mondaq.com/india/advertising-marketing-branding/606974/surrogate-advertisements-in-india)
- Documented liquor-linked surrogate brand examples: Bagpiper (club soda), Officer's Choice (playing cards), Bacardi (music/"Blast" CDs), Seagram's Imperial Blue (music CDs), McDowell's/Kingfisher (soda, mineral/packaged drinking water, and in Kingfisher's case cross-promotion via Kingfisher Airlines) — [Wikipedia: Surrogate advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogate_advertising); [indiantelevision.com: "Surrogate liquor advertising: Time for change?"](https://www.indiantelevision.com/mam/marketing/mam/surrogate-liquor-advertising-time-for-change-171002)
- Documented tobacco/pan-masala-linked surrogate examples: Vimal, Rajnigandha, and Kamla Pasand pan masala brands advertise cardamom/mouth-freshener products using identical brand names, packaging colors, and jingles as their gutka/pan masala lines — [Wikipedia: Surrogate advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogate_advertising); FM radio study below
- A peer-reviewed study (Singh et al., *Frontiers in Public Health*, vol. 10, Oct 14, 2022) monitored 162 hours of FM radio across 9 channels in Delhi NCR (July–August 2021) and documented 286 instances of surrogate tobacco advertisements; Vimal accounted for 82% of these ads, Kamla Pasand 10%, and Rajshree 8% — all promoted as cardamom/mouth-freshener products. Bollywood celebrity voice-overs were used, more than half of voice-overs were female (suggesting deliberate targeting), slogans included "sab banenge follower" and "tashan ka jashan." No such ads were found on government-owned radio channels, only on private ones — [Frontiers in Public Health, 10.3389/fpubh.2022.999552](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.999552/full)
- India has an estimated ~200 million smokeless-tobacco users, roughly two-thirds of global smokeless tobacco consumption, framing the public-health stakes of pan-masala surrogate advertising — [Frontiers in Public Health, 10.3389/fpubh.2022.999552](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.999552/full)
- Recent (2026) controversy: the Maharashtra Food and Drug Administration issued show-cause notices to Shah Rukh Khan, Ajay Devgn, and Tiger Shroff over their Vimal Elaichi endorsement campaign, alleging it amounts to surrogate/indirect promotion of Vimal Pan Masala (a tobacco-linked product banned in Maharashtra); the actors face a potential fine of up to Rs. 10 lakh if their responses are found unsatisfactory — [Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-marketing/srk-ajay-devgn-and-tiger-shroff-could-face-%E2%82%B910-lakh-fine-over-vimal-elaichi-ads-says-maharashtra-fda-chief-108068.htm); [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/srk-ajay-devgn-and-tiger-shroff-get-an-fda-notice-what-the-vimal-elaichi-row-means-and-what-the-law-says)
- A survey cited in secondary sources found that 42 of 50 respondents could correctly identify the actual (restricted) liquor or tobacco product being referenced behind a surrogate ad, used as evidence that surrogate ads are effective at achieving brand recall despite nominally advertising a different, legal product — [Wikipedia: Surrogate advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogate_advertising) (original study/survey source not independently verified — treat with caution as it is cited only secondhand)
- Historical origin anecdote: Azad Bidi's sponsorship of an international cricket match is cited as early evidence of surrogate-style promotion in India, preceding the Cable TV Regulation Act's formal ban on liquor/cigarette ads — [Wikipedia: Surrogate advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogate_advertising) (unverified primary source; flagged as secondary-source claim)
- Under Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) guidelines issued in June 2022, surrogate advertising was explicitly prohibited; on October 7, 2022, the consumer affairs ministry issued notices to six brands for violations — [Wikipedia: Surrogate advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrogate_advertising) — this should be cross-checked against the primary CCPA Guidelines on Prevention of Misleading Advertisements, 2022 (see next section) since Wikipedia is a secondary/tertiary source.

### Inferences
- The consistent pattern across both liquor and pan-masala/tobacco surrogate ads is retention of brand name, logo, color scheme, jingle, and (increasingly) the same celebrity endorser as the restricted product — meaning any computational detector would need to model brand/visual identity continuity across product categories (e.g., "soda ad visually identical to liquor ad branding") rather than relying on textual/keyword cues about the ostensible product (soda, elaichi, water) alone.
- The FM radio study's finding that surrogate tobacco ads appear only on private channels and not government-owned ones suggests medium-of-broadcast (state vs. private, or regulated vs. less-regulated platforms) is itself a usable signal/feature for detection or risk-scoring.

## What guidelines has ASCI issued on surrogate advertising and influencer/health-harm product ads?

### Takeaway
ASCI's self-regulatory Code (not law) defines surrogate advertising and, since December 2023, has layered on detailed "Guidelines for Qualification of Brand Extension" that set minimum sales-turnover and verification thresholds a brand extension must meet to avoid being classified as a surrogate ad; ASCI has also been coordinating directly with the government's Department of Consumer Affairs (DoCA) on enforcement in restricted categories (alcohol, tobacco, online betting/gambling).

### Cited Findings
- ASCI's self-regulatory framework prohibits "the use of surrogate advertisements to promote alcohol or any other prohibited product indirectly," defining it as any advertisement for a permitted good/service that circumvents a legal prohibition/restriction by portraying itself as an ad for something unrestricted — [Lexology: "Legal analysis of tobacco and alcohol advertising laws in India"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=c3625316-71db-492b-b896-a5bd471614ed)
- Under ASCI's "Guidelines for Qualification of Brand Extension," a brand present in market for more than 2 years must show sales turnover exceeding Rs. 5 crore/annum nationally OR Rs. 1 crore/annum per state where distributed, verified by an independent certificate (e.g., from NielsenIQ, a category industry association, or a chartered accountant), to be considered a genuine (non-surrogate) brand extension — [ASCI Guidelines for Qualification of Brand Extension PDF](https://www.ascionline.in/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Guidelines-for-Qualification-of-brand-extension-1.pdf)
- For brand extensions less than 2 years old, qualification requires either: net sales turnover of Rs. 20 lakh/month from launch; OR exclusive fixed-asset investment in the extension of at least Rs. 10 crore; OR turnover exceeding 10% of the parent brand's turnover in the restricted category (including sub-brands in that category) — [ASCI Guidelines for Qualification of Brand Extension PDF](https://www.ascionline.in/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Guidelines-for-Qualification-of-brand-extension-1.pdf)
- If these quantitative thresholds are not met, the extension is "not considered a genuine brand extension" under the ASCI Code and is instead treated as a surrogate — [BW Marketing World: "ASCI Fortifies Guidelines For Qualification Of Brand Extension Of Restricted Categories"](https://www.bwmarketingworld.com/article/asci-fortifies-guidelines-for-qualification-of-brand-extension-of-restricted-categories-502206)
- ASCI and the Department of Consumer Affairs (DoCA) jointly convened an interactive stakeholder consultation on February 22, 2024 in Mumbai, specifically targeting industry players in restricted categories (alcohol, tobacco, gambling/betting) — [afaqs.com coverage](https://www.afaqs.com/news/advertising/asci-consults-with-department-of-consumer-affairs-on-surrogate-ads)
- In the three years preceding that consultation, ASCI processed complaints against 49 ads for potential violation of the brand-extension qualification guidelines: 36 were alcohol-related, 12 were betting-platform ads, and 1 was a pan masala ad — [afaqs.com coverage](https://www.afaqs.com/news/advertising/asci-consults-with-department-of-consumer-affairs-on-surrogate-ads)

### Inferences
- ASCI's qualification test is fundamentally financial/documentary (sales turnover, independent audit certificates) rather than content-based — meaning ASCI's own framework does not define surrogate advertising by visual/textual ad features at all; a computational ad-classification system cannot replicate ASCI's compliance test directly from ad content alone, since the same ad creative could be legal or illegal depending on the brand's underlying (non-public, audited) sales data. This is a structural reason automated detection of surrogate ads is hard: the "ground truth" label depends on off-screen commercial facts, not just on-screen content.
- The low volume of ASCI's own enforcement (49 ads actioned over 3 years, almost entirely alcohol) relative to the apparent prevalence found by content-analysis studies (e.g., 286 FM radio instances in just 2 months in one city) suggests ASCI's self-regulatory, complaint-driven enforcement captures only a small fraction of actual surrogate advertising activity.
- I did not find ASCI guidelines specifically targeting influencer-endorsed surrogate/health-harm ads as a distinct named category (separate from its general 2021 influencer/celebrity disclosure guidelines) in this research pass — this is flagged as a gap below.

### Gaps
- Did not retrieve ASCI's specific 2021 "Guidelines for Influencer Advertising on Digital Media" text or confirm whether/how it cross-references surrogate-advertising rules for influencer content specifically (e.g., whether influencers promoting "elaichi"/soda brands trigger additional disclosure duties). This should be pursued with a direct query against ascionline.in if time allows.

## Has any academic, legal, or policy analysis attempted to systematically identify or quantify surrogate advertising in Indian media? Any computational/data-driven attempts?

### Takeaway
There is a small but real body of academic/public-health literature that manually content-analyzes Indian media (films, FM radio) for tobacco imagery and surrogate advertising using structured coding protocols, but no computational/machine-learning system specifically built to detect surrogate advertising in Indian media was found in this research pass — this appears to be an open gap in the literature.

### Cited Findings
- Singh et al. (*Frontiers in Public Health*, 2022) is a rare quantitative, systematic content-analysis study: researchers manually monitored 162 hours of FM radio across 9 Delhi NCR channels over July–August 2021 and coded 286 distinct surrogate tobacco-ad instances by brand, voice-over gender, and slogan content — a reproducible manual-coding methodology, not an automated/ML one — [Frontiers in Public Health, 10.3389/fpubh.2022.999552](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.999552/full)
- A Tobacco Control journal study used structured content analysis (manual coding against a published protocol) of 47 Indian films to quantify tobacco imagery (72% of films) and legal compliance (0% fully compliant) — again a manual/human-coded methodology — [PMC6952842](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6952842/)
- A trend-analysis study of Bollywood films 2006–2017 quantified tobacco "incidents" per film over time to measure before/after effects of the 2012 Tobacco-Free Film and TV Rules, again via manual coding of film content rather than automated detection — [WHO SEARO-hosted reprint of Tobacco Control study](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/searo/india/health-topic-pdf/tobacco/changes-in-tobacco-depictions-after-implementation-of-tobacco-free-film-and-tv-rules-_tobacco-control.pdf)
- Legal/management-studies literature (e.g., Ayon Sarkar, *Management and Labour Studies*, Vol. 36, No. 3, 2011, DOI 10.1177/0258042X1103600305) analyzes surrogate advertising primarily as a legal/regulatory-circumvention phenomenon with case-study examples, not as a quantitative or computational detection exercise — [IJSDR paper summarizing/citing this literature](https://ijsdr.org/papers/IJSDR2501062.pdf)
- A broad web search specifically for "computational detection surrogate advertisement India machine learning" returned no papers on ML/computational detection of surrogate advertising; results instead surfaced unrelated ad-fraud/click-fraud detection literature and generic adversarial-ML "surrogate model" papers (a different, unrelated use of the word "surrogate" in ML terminology) — this is a meaningful negative finding — [WebSearch results, no directly relevant source found]
- An observational study (Negi, Gill, Maharjan, Sinha, Puri, Mallik, Mullin, Tullu, Murukutla; *Tobacco Prevention & Cessation* journal family, 2023) examined implementation of the Tobacco-Free Film and Television Policy in India, likely via systematic observational/coding methodology, though full methodological detail was not extracted in this pass — [DOI 10.1177/1179173X231205377](https://doi.org/10.1177/1179173x231205377)

### Inferences
- The absence of any computational/ML detection literature specific to Indian surrogate advertising — despite an active public-health research community manually content-coding tobacco imagery in film/radio — strongly suggests that automated/computational classification of surrogate ads is a genuinely novel or underexplored research problem in the Indian context, not one with existing benchmark datasets or published models to build on. Any new project in this space would likely be among the first computational attempts, rather than one competing with/building on prior ML work.
- Existing academic methodology is entirely manual/human-coded (content analysis against published coding protocols for tobacco imagery), which could serve as a useful *labeling scheme/taxonomy* (e.g., presence of health spot, static warning, disclaimer, tobacco-use incident count) for training a future classifier, even though no such classifier currently exists in the literature found.

### Gaps
- No computational/data-driven (ML, NLP, computer vision) published work specifically targeting detection of surrogate advertising in Indian media was found; this should be explicitly reported to the report-writer as an identified gap in the literature, not an oversight in this research.
- Did not obtain full-text access to the Ayon Sarkar (2011) *Management and Labour Studies* paper or the Negi et al. (2023) *Tobacco Prevention & Cessation*-family paper beyond abstract-level summaries (via secondary WebFetch synthesis); a researcher with journal database access could extract more granular case-study/methodology detail from these if needed.
- Did not search specifically for non-English-language (e.g., Hindi-language policy journals, UPSC/India-specific policy think-tank reports beyond those surfaced) academic treatments, which may contain additional quantitative attempts not indexed in English-language web search.

## What enforcement challenges exist (why is this hard to regulate/detect today)?

### Takeaway
Enforcement of both direct tobacco/alcohol ad bans and surrogate-advertising rules in India is hampered by weak/outdated penalties relative to inflation, fragmented jurisdiction across multiple regulators (COTPA authorities, State FDAs, CCPA, ASCI, FSSAI), and a structural legal ambiguity in which companies can argue a surrogate product is genuinely independent — courts have sometimes accepted this where regulators could not prove the product contained the restricted substance or that the campaign legally constituted indirect promotion.

### Cited Findings
- The core enforcement problem "is not the absence of a law but in its enforcement and commercial tactics developed around it" — surrogate advertising is characterized as a deliberate regulatory loophole exploited despite formal advertising bans — [ETV Bharat: "India's Tobacco Crisis: Surrogate Advertising Raises Questions About Social Responsibilities Of Celebrities"](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/bharat/surrogate-advertising-raises-questions-about-social-responsibilities-of-celebrities-enn26091903772)
- A major enforcement weakness is that COTPA's statutory penalties (fines of Rs. 1,000–5,000, per Section 5) have not been adjusted for inflation since 2003 and are "readily affordable," failing to act as a meaningful deterrent for well-resourced tobacco/pan-masala/liquor companies — [Open synthesis of multiple sources incl. legacyias.com and ETV Bharat coverage](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/bharat/surrogate-advertising-raises-questions-about-social-responsibilities-of-celebrities-enn26091903772)
- Regulatory authority over this space is fragmented across the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), State Food and Drug Administrations (as in the Maharashtra FDA's Vimal Elaichi action), FSSAI (for pan masala/food-safety angles), and ASCI (self-regulatory, non-statutory) — creating overlapping jurisdiction and inconsistent enforcement outcomes — [WebSearch synthesis of ResearchGate "Enforcement of COTPA in India" and related sources](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381522925_Enforcement_of_COTPA_in_India-_current_status_challenges_and_solutions)
- Courts have in some instances allowed surrogate-style advertising to continue where regulators could not establish that the advertised item actually contained the restricted substance (e.g., tobacco) or that the campaign legally qualified as indirect/surrogate promotion under the applicable statute — illustrating a legal-proof burden that favors advertisers — [WebSearch synthesis, enforcement-challenges query]
- Under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, Section 2(28) defines "misleading advertisement" broadly (false description, false guarantees, implying unfair trade practice, concealing material information), and the CCPA (established under Section 10) notified "Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements, 2022" with penalties up to Rs. 10 lakh for a first violation and Rs. 50 lakh for subsequent violations, plus power to bar an endorser from making endorsements for 1 year (first violation) to 3 years (repeat) — [PIB press release on the 2022 CCPA Guidelines](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1832906&reg=48&lang=2); WebSearch synthesis of related secondary coverage
- A continued-sale investigative report found gutka (banned nationally as a manufactured product under the Food Safety and Standards Act/regulations) remains widely sold despite nominal bans, due to loopholes in state-level enforcement orders and a "failing enforcement regime" — illustrating that even outright-banned products (not just advertising) suffer from the same enforcement weakness, which compounds the surrogate-advertising problem since the underlying product's availability is itself inconsistently enforced — [Open: "If Gutkha is Banned Across India, Why is it Still Sold?"](https://openthemagazine.com/india/if-gutkha-is-banned-across-india-why-is-it-still-sold-inside-loopholes-state-orders-and-a-failing-enforcement-regime)

### Inferences
- Because enforcement is complaint-driven (ASCI) or triggered by ad-hoc state agency action (as in the 2026 Maharashtra FDA/Vimal Elaichi case) rather than proactive/systematic monitoring, there is no continuously operating institutional mechanism in India comprehensively scanning broadcast/digital media for surrogate ads — this is itself the clearest evidence/motivation for why an automated detection system would be valuable, and why none appears to exist yet (per the literature gap found above).
- The legal ambiguity point (courts sometimes siding with advertisers absent proof the surrogate product contains the restricted substance) implies that any detection system's output would need to be understood as a risk/flagging signal rather than a legal determination — final classification of "is this a surrogate ad" remains, under current Indian law, inherently a fact-specific/financial-data determination (per the ASCI turnover-threshold test) rather than a purely content-based one.
